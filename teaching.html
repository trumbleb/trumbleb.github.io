Teaching philosophy

During the archival phase of my research, I read a town report from 1962 where a New Hampshire school superintendent defended the value of education not just for individual benefit, but to assist future citizens to “add to the general welfare” and “be able to live well with his [sic] fellows so that life shall mean no less, and may mean more to his fellows because he is one of them,” the highest purpose of education being “for the benefit of both the individual and the group” and “good citizenship.” This emphasis on service is why I teach. Education helps us learn from the past, think critically, and improve our station, not just for our own satisfaction, but for the good of our community. I take these words to heart and have worked as an educator for most of my adult life.
At the University of Washington, I have been employed as the lead instructor for three courses and as a teaching assistant for ten additional courses. In recognition of my work as an educator, I was awarded the 2026 Graduate Student Teaching Award. Before beginning my PhD, I earned a Master of Arts in Teaching and taught high school social studies for several years, where I received commendations for my teaching and inclusive, rigorous, civic pedagogy.
The University of Washington is a majority-minority institution, and I taught at a rural Title-I high school—I have succeeded in both careers and served students across wide demographic differences. I challenge students not with ideas they already are comfortable with, but push them to think beyond their pre-conceived notions. I scaffold to encourage discussion and deliberation in my classroom, teaching students to consider diverse ideas and arguments. I center content learning, while building towards creative application thereof. Education is a pathway to better, fulfilling lives. Pedagogy is central to my calling as an academic.
